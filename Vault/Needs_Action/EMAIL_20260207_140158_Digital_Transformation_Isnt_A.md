Purchasing software is not an equivalent of digital transformation. Companies frequently launch a new CRM, analytics platform, or AI-driven automation, and suddenly the office is talking about digital progress, even if the real problems remain untouched. Three weeks since then, their sales team continues to work with spreadsheets. There are still cases where days are being used to solve customer complaints. Numbers are something that the leadership cannot rely on. And why transformation comes to feel, according to none, existent. This is the bitter reality; tools are not difficult. Real change is hard. Tools Don’t Fix Broken Businesses The software is tempting as it can be seen. You are able to purchase it and display it in a meeting and check a box. Oh, we are modernizing, you see. However, conflicting ownership will not be solved by any CRM. There will not be any analytics dashboard to accelerate the decision-making process when your group is always awaiting approvals. No automation tool would resolve any process that is not logical. Buying tools is the easy part. It is the answer to the real issues which differentiates those companies that talk about change and also those who practice change. A Scenario You Know All Too Well Consider a medium sized business that has a problem with delayed deliveries. Clients are frustrated. Teams are frustrated. Deadlines are missed. Technology is the source of the blame in leadership, and thus they purchase a project management tool. The tool is available six weeks later. However, the truth remains the same: developers do not finish their tasks in time, project managers continue to follow-up on Slack, and sales promises schedules, which are not to be. The tool is operational, yet delivery remains slow. Why? The actual problems were never discussed: undefined ownership, non-realistic promises, and lack of a collective understanding of what was done. No software fixes that. Transformation Starts With Brutal Honesty Any real digital transformation follows one uncomfortable question: “What’s actually broken here?” Not “Which tool are we missing?” Not what is trending on LinkedIn? What is the competition doing? Where do decisions stall? Where is time wasted? What are customers experiencing friction on? These are the first things to answer, and you have your opportunities of changing. Take an example of a logistics company that we were engaged with. They believed that the issue was obsolete technology. Upon further investigation, it became clear: data existed in five systems, no one was trusted, drivers used to report delays manually, operations updated schedules at the end of the day and finance crunch numbers days after delivery. More software was not required by them. One source of truth and a working process that actually worked was what they required. After that was corrected, the addition of the tools caused the needle to move in fact. Tools Can Be a Distraction I have also witnessed it too much: firms purchase automation or analytics software, and everybody is on the move now, yet nothing is improved. Dashboards are constructed on data that nobody has trust. Automation accelerates processes that do not even have to be. CRMs become dumping grounds. Human beings begin accusing the technology. It was never an issue of the tool, it was the process of work, the ownership and decisions which no one was in line with. Real Transformation Is Quiet — and Hard It’s not flashy. It does not make your LinkedIn feed attractive. It’s messy and uncomfortable. It begins by tracing the way work really occurs, not the way you wish that it occurred. It makes one ask the question of who, what, where and why. It will eliminate redundant processes and then automate the rest. A single customer response time was decreased by 40 percent in one of our retail undertakings without purchasing a new item. They just re-architectured the flow of queries through the sales, support and the operations. It was at that point that software contributed to the enhancement.
